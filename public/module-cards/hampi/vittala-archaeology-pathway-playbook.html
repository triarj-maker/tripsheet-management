Vittala - Archaeology Pathway - Playbook

VITTALA ARCHAEOLOGY PLAYBOOK

Purpose

To help students use the Vijaya Vittala Temple as archaeological evidence. Through guided observation and structured inquiry, students examine conservation, sculpture, architectural claims and multiple sources of evidence. The module repeatedly asks students to distinguish between what they can observe, what they infer, and how strongly the available evidence supports an interpretation.

Module Snapshot

Student Group: Grades 7–10

Module Type: Vittala - Monuments as Evidence

Total Duration: 140 minutes

Group Size: 20 students / multiple groups of 20 for bigger batches

Team: 1 Guide per group

Route:
Vittala Parking → Buggy Line → Vittala Entrance / Gopura → Kalyana Mantapa → Ranga Mantapa → Stone Chariot → Purandara Dasa Mantapa

List of Sections

  1. Vittala Parking & Buggy Transfer
  2. Vittala Entrance — Gopura
  3. Kalyana Mantapa
  4. Ranga Mantapa
  5. Stone Chariot
  6. Purandara Dasa Mantapa
  7. Return Journey — Purandara Dasa Mantapa to Bus

Module Safety / Logistics

  • Maintain the group together throughout the visit and conduct headcounts at key transition points.
  • Facilitators manage student movement, positioning and engagement throughout the module.
  • Students must follow the designated movement route and instructions during bus and buggy transfers.
  • At the buggy line, ensure students remain together and prevent unfamiliar visitors from entering the student group.
  • One adult should accompany each buggy.
  • Students must not touch, climb, lean on, sit on or place objects on protected monuments.
  • Musical pillars and other architectural elements must not be tapped, struck or physically tested.
  • Maintain appropriate conduct around the temple complex and at the river-side Purandara Dasa Mantapa.
  • Facilitators should ensure the guide can deliver the intended narrative without unnecessary interruptions while intervening when safety or student engagement requires it.
  • The Stone Chariot section may be led by the assigned Guide or by the Lead Facilitator taking guide groups in staggered slots.
  • If the Lead Facilitator is delivering the Stone Chariot section, sequence guide groups so each group receives a staggered slot. If Guides are delivering the section independently, groups may continue on their own route sequence.

Section 1. Vittala Parking & Buggy Transfer

Section Details

Duration: 30 mins

Led by: Lead Facilitator

Location

Vittala parking area → buggy boarding area

Intent of the Section

  • Move the group efficiently from the bus to the Vittala entrance.
  • Ensure safe and orderly buggy boarding and transfer.

All Facilitators: Script

At the parking / before the buggy line

“Everyone, please gather for announcements. We would like to collectively make some announcements and give your instructions”

Lead Facilitator Script

Once the group is together in a shaded area

“Hello everyone. We’ll shortly be taking the electric buggies to the Vijaya Vittala Temple, which is about a kilometre and a half from here.

While you are in the buggy, please remain seated at all times, do not stand up, and maintain discipline throughout the ride.

When you reach the other end, one of our facilitators will be waiting to receive you and escort you to the area where we will all gather again.

At the parking / before the buggy line

  • Conduct a quick headcount.
  • Hand the school’s required letter/documentation to the guide.
  • Confirm that the guide is present and ready to receive the group.
  • Move the students towards the buggy boarding area without unnecessary delays.

At the buggy line

  • Monitor the movement of students into the buggies.

  • Confirm that one adult is present in each buggy.
  • Ensure students do not rush ahead or leave the line.
  • The adult travelling in each buggy is responsible for ensuring that the students remain together and are safely moved to the drop-off point near the Vittala entrance.

Section 2. Vittala Entrance — Gopura

Section Details

Duration: 15 mins

Led by: Lead Facilitator + Guide

Academic Focus: Gopura architecture, conservation, preservation, restoration, authenticity and historical accuracy

Location

Vittala Temple entrance / Gopura

Intent of the Section

To introduce students to the idea that archaeological monuments are sources of evidence that need to be conserved carefully. Students examine the gopura and its partially repaired condition before considering the difficult choices involved in preserving an ancient structure without compromising its authenticity or historical information.

Lead Facilitator Script

"Before we split into our guide groups, I want to give you one idea that will help you through the whole Vittala visit."

"Your guides are not always going to tell you the answer first. Sometimes they will ask you to look, notice something, and tell them what you think."

"When that happens, start with what you can actually see."

"For example, if I say, 'That wall is made of stone,' I am making an observation. I can point to the evidence."

"If I say, 'That wall must have taken a lot of skilled labour to build,' I have gone one step further. I am interpreting what the evidence might mean."

"So what is the difference between an observation and interpretation?"

Take 1-2 responses.

"Exactly. Both are very useful in the study of an archaeological or historical site”

"Through our time at Vittala, keep asking yourself two questions: What can I see? And what might that evidence mean? We are trying to teach you an important archaeological skill - observing and interpreting an archaeological site"

The facilitator then hands over the group to the guide. Introduce the guides to their groups "Now we will divide you into our guide groups. Stay with your assigned guide as you move through the Vittala sections."

“Your guide is going to take us through the site, over to you”

Guide Script

Guide - First Observation

"Before I tell you anything about this place, I want you to look at this structure for a moment."

"What are you looking at?"

Take up to 3 responses. Choose students from raised hands.

If useful, use one or two prompts only:

"What do you notice about its size?"

"What materials can you identify?"

Do not draw attention to repaired portions yet.

"This structure is hundreds of years old. Over time, parts of it have worn out, broken and been repaired."

"So here is an important question: when an old monument is damaged, what should we do with it?"

Guide - Conservation Problem

"Imagine that you are responsible for protecting this monument. Some parts are damaged. Some parts are missing. What should you do?"

Take 2-3 responses.

"Okay. But here's where it becomes complicated."

"Suppose you replace a damaged part with a new one that looks exactly like the original. The monument looks more complete."

"But is the new piece actually part of the original historical evidence?"

Pause briefly. Take 1-2 responses only if hands go up immediately.

"And if you don't repair anything, the structure might continue to deteriorate."

"So which is more important here: keeping the monument physically intact, or keeping its original material intact?"

Take up to 2 responses. Do not try to resolve every answer.

Guide - Ship of Theseus

"Let's take an example."

"Imagine you have a ship. One plank becomes damaged, so you replace it. Then another plank gets damaged, and you replace that one too. Then another."

"Eventually, every original part of the ship has been replaced."

"Do you still have the same ship, or is it an entirely new ship?"

Take 2-3 responses.

"There is no simple answer to this question."

"Now apply the same question to an ancient monument. If we keep replacing damaged parts with new ones until the monument looks complete again, have we preserved the original monument, or have we created a reconstruction of it?"

Guide - Vittala Gopura Case

"Now look at this gopura again. This time, look at it as someone thinking about conservation."

"Do all portions look the same?"

Give students time to look. Take 2-3 observations.

If they need help, ask them to compare the surface, finish, weathering and completeness of different portions. Do not point out the answer immediately.

"Can you identify any portions that may have been worked on later? What makes you think that?"

Take 1-2 responses.

"Some portions of this gopura show later conservation and reconstruction work, while other portions preserve much more weathered older fabric."

"You can see differences in the surface, finish, completeness and the styling of the stucco figures."

"Conservation and restoration work was undertaken at monuments across Hampi during the twentieth century. Work on damaged architectural and stucco elements sometimes involved recreating missing portions."

"At Vittala, a reconstruction and conservation effort included new stucco work to repair damaged portions of the gopura."

"The Archaeological Survey of India eventually halted that work following pressure from civil society and local people because the quality and finish of the restoration was considered far from the original."

"That creates a problem for archaeologists and conservators: a reconstruction may make a monument look more complete, but the new work is not original Vijayanagara-period evidence."

Guide - Land the Conservation Idea

"Conservation is not about making an old building look new again."

"It means protecting what is still there, and being careful about what we repair, replace or rebuild."

"We also need to know which parts are original and which parts were added later."

"Before any repair, conservators record the monument carefully. Today, tools like 3D surveys can create a detailed record of how the monument looks before work begins."

"So the question is not just, 'How do we fix it?'"

"It is, 'How do we protect this monument without losing the clues it gives us about the past?'"

Guide - Gopura Orientation

"Before we move on, let's identify the structure itself."

"This is a gopura, an entrance gateway to the main core area of the temple complex. Walking through it gives access to the walled inner courtyard that contains the core temple structures."

"This gopura is close to 50 feet high, roughly the height of a four- to five-storey building today. At Vittala there are three gopuras, towards the east, north and south."

"At our next stop, we’ll look at the sculptures and ask: what can they tell us about the people who lived here long ago?"

Facilitator Prompts

Use these only when needed. Do not ask all of them. The main purpose is to help students look carefully, think about conservation, and support their answers with what they can see.

First Observation

  • “What do you notice?”
  • “What makes you say that?”

Conservation Problem

  • “If you were responsible for this monument, what would you do?”
  • “What might happen if we replace too much of the original?”
  • “What might happen if we do nothing?”

Ship of Theseus

  • “If every old part is replaced with a new part, is it still the same monument?”

Return to the Gopura

  • “Do all parts of the gopura look the same?”
  • “What differences can you see?”
  • “Which parts may have been repaired later? What makes you think that?”

Land the Idea

  • “How can we protect this monument without losing the clues it gives us about the past?”

Section 3. Kalyana Mantapa

Section Details

Duration: 10 mins

Led by: Guide

Academic Focus: Architecture, sculpture, social structure, cultural diversity, military organisation and political identity

Location

Kalyana Mantapa, Vijaya Vittala Temple complex

Intent of the Section

To teach students how architectural and sculptural features can be interpreted as evidence about the society that created them.

The section focuses on society and identity: who were the people represented in the architecture, what did their clothing and equipment communicate, and what can these representations tell us about the Vijayanagara kingdom?

Guide Script

Guide - Orientation

"This is the Kalyana Mantapa, traditionally understood as a ceremonial wedding hall associated with the temple."

"Even before we look at the sculptures, a structure like this tells us something. It required materials, labour, knowledge and skilled craftsmanship organised on a very large scale."

"But for the next few minutes, we are going to focus on the sculptures and ask: what can they tell us about the people represented here?"

Guide - Yali Makara Pillars

Direct students toward a clear Yali pillar.

"This is a mythical creature called a Yali. Instead of looking at it as one animal, break it into parts. What different animal features can you see?"

Take up to 4 responses. If needed, draw attention to the mane, body, ears and hind legs or talons.

"We can see a mane like a lion, a body like a horse, pointed ears like a deer, and hind legs with talon-like features like an eagle."

"Those are things we can observe."

"Now let's interpret them. The Yali brings together the courage and leadership of a lion, the responsiveness of a deer, the dependability of a horse, and the quickness of an eagle."

"So the Yali becomes a symbol of the qualities of a powerful warrior and protector."

"A Yali is not evidence that such an animal really existed. It is evidence of an idea that people chose to show in their architecture."

Guide - The Riders and Protection

Direct students to two adjacent Yali pillars with clearly visible mounted riders.

"Now compare the riders on these two Yalis. Look at them side by side."

"What differences can you see in their clothing, hairstyle or headgear, and weapons?"

Take 2-3 responses. If needed, ask students to compare one feature at a time.

"Good. They are both warriors, but they are not shown in exactly the same way."

"These decorated soldiers represent the diverse warlords of Vijayanagara, coming from different parts of the empire and from regions beyond it."

"Now look at the whole image. Each warlord is shown mounted on a powerful Yali, with the Yali's front legs raised."

"The repeated image presents the army of Vijayanagara as protectors of the kingdom, its temples, sacred spaces and the deity."

"The Yali stands for qualities such as courage, leadership, responsiveness, dependability and speed. By placing the warrior on the Yali, the sculpture links those qualities to the military protector."

Guide - Land the Idea

"Notice what we did here. First we looked at what was actually carved. Then we asked what those details might mean."

"That is how sculpture can become evidence about people and ideas from the past."

"At our next stop, we will start with a claim and see how different kinds of evidence can help us investigate it."

Section 4. Ranga Mantapa

Section Details

Duration: 15 mins

Led by: Guide

Academic Focus: Archaeological inquiry, multiple sources of evidence, scientific investigation, acoustics and interpretation

Central Question: What evidence can tell us what this hall was used for, and whether its pillars were deliberately designed to produce sound?

Materials: Tuning fork with rubber activator + Ranga Mantapa sculpture visual aid

Location

Ranga Mantapa / 56-pillared hall, Vijaya Vittala Temple complex

Intent of the Section

To use the Ranga Mantapa to show how archaeologists build an interpretation by combining different kinds of evidence. Students investigate two linked questions: what happened in this hall, and whether its pillars were deliberately designed to produce sound.

The section should land one idea: archaeology studies the past through evidence, and scientific methods can help us test what that evidence means.

Guide Script

Guide - Orientation

"This is the Ranga Mantapa. The word Ranga is connected with performance, stage and art."

"This hall has 56 main pillars. Many have smaller stone columns carved within them."

"At this stop, we are going to investigate two questions: what happened in this hall, and why are these called musical pillars?"

Guide - The Musical-Pillar Claim

Direct students towards the pillars that are clearly visible from the permitted viewing position.

"These are popularly called the musical pillars. The claim is that some of these smaller stone columns can produce different sounds when struck."

"But we cannot strike them ourselves. This is a protected monument."

"So how can we investigate the claim without touching the pillars?"

Take 2-3 responses. Accept simple suggestions such as looking carefully, using an experiment, studying other evidence or using scientific research.

"Good. We can observe the monument, experiment with something else, look at other evidence, and use research."

Guide - Evidence 1: Pillar Form

"Look carefully at the smaller columns. Are they all the same?"

Take 1-2 observations.

"Some are longer, some shorter. Some are thicker, some thinner. Their shapes also vary."

"The smaller columns are carved as part of the same stone block as the larger pillar."

"These are things we can observe. But different shapes alone do not prove that the columns were designed to make different sounds."

Guide - Evidence 2: Vibration and Resonance

Use the tuning fork only with the rubber activator. Do not strike it on the monument or any protected stone.

"This is a tuning fork. When I activate it with the rubber activator, I give it energy. That energy makes the two prongs move back and forth."

Activate the tuning fork.

"What do you think is producing the sound?"

Take 1-2 responses.

"That movement is vibration. The vibrating prongs push and pull the air around them. Those pressure changes travel through the air, and we hear them as sound."

"Now notice something important. A tuning fork is designed with prongs of a particular length and thickness. Those dimensions affect how fast the prongs naturally vibrate."

If two tuning forks of different frequencies are available, demonstrate them briefly.

"If two tuning forks have prongs of different lengths or thicknesses, they vibrate at different frequencies. Frequency simply means how many times something vibrates in a second. A different frequency gives us a different pitch, or sound."

"When an object vibrates strongly at one of its natural frequencies, we call that resonance. So the tuning fork has shown us one important scientific idea: the shape, length and thickness of an object affect how it vibrates and the sound it produces."

Guide - Evidence 3: Why the Stone Matters

"The shape of the pillar is one part of the story. The other part is the stone itself."

"Granite is made up of different kinds of minerals. Researchers studying the granite around Hampi found that it contains a mineral called pink feldspar in a higher proportion than is normally found in granite."

"Pink feldspar has an interesting property. The way this mineral is structured allows vibration to travel through the stone more easily without getting dampened quickly. In many other stones, the way the minerals are structured causes the energy to dissipate quickly and vibration to dampen.

"So the presence of more pink feldspar gives the granite around Hampi a stronger resonant property. In simple terms, vibration can travel through the stone more easily and last longer."

"Now we can put the two pieces together. The tuning fork showed us that the shape and thickness of an object affect the frequency at which it vibrates and therefore the sound it produces. The Hampi granite gives us the other part: the material itself allows vibration to travel and continue."

"So the musical-pillar claim now has a scientific basis worth investigating: the columns have different forms that can affect how they vibrate, and they are made from a stone that can carry and sustain those vibrations well."

"But science is only one kind of evidence available to us here. Let us now look at another kind of evidence: the sculptures in this hall."

Guide - Evidence 4: Sculptures and the Function of the Hall

Use the Ranga Mantapa sculpture visual aid. It should show clear close-up images of the mridangam player, tala or cymbal player, damaru player and classical dancer because these sculptures are difficult for students to see clearly from the viewing position.

"Now let us investigate our other question: what was this hall used for?"

Show the visual aid.

"These are sculptures from inside this hall. What activities can you see?"

Take 2-3 responses.

"We can see musicians playing instruments such as the mridangam, tala or cymbals and damaru. We can also see a classical dancer."

"What might that tell us about what happened in this hall?"

Take 1-2 responses.

"This gives us strong evidence that music, dance and performance were important activities in this space."

Guide - Evidence 5: Sculptures and Reported Sounds

"Now look at two of those musician sculptures again."

"Observers have reported that columns associated with the cymbal player produce higher-pitched sounds, while columns associated with the mridangam player produce deeper sounds."

"That is interesting because the image on the pillar and the reported quality of the sound seem to match."

Guide - Evidence 6: Historical Knowledge

"We can also ask another question: could the builders have known how stone and shape affect sound?"

"Historical texts on architecture and sculpting describe testing stone for qualities including sound. Some later texts also discuss how different pillar shapes can produce different kinds of sound."

"People in the wider Hampi region had also used naturally resonant rocks as rock gongs long before Vijayanagara."

"So knowledge of stone and sound existed. That makes the idea of deliberate acoustic design worth investigating."

Guide - Modern Scientific Research

"Today researchers can go further. They can measure the columns, study the stone, record sound frequencies, study vibrations and compare different structures."

"The study of sound in archaeological and historical places is called archaeoacoustics."

"Archaeology is not only about old stories. It studies the past through material evidence and uses scientific methods to test ideas."

Guide - Build the Evidence Stack

"Let us put all our evidence together."

Count the evidence on your fingers as you speak.

"One: the physical design of the pillars."

"Two: what vibration and resonance tell us about sound."

"Three: the mineral composition and crystal structure of the stone, including pink feldspar."

"Four: sculptures of musicians and dancers that tell us about the function of the hall."

"Five: reported links between some musician sculptures and the sounds of their columns."

"Six: historical knowledge about stone, shape and sound."

"Does any one of these pieces prove everything?"

Take 1-2 responses.

"No. But when several different kinds of evidence point in the same direction, our interpretation becomes stronger."

Guide - Land the Idea

"So what can we say about our first question: what happened in this hall?"

"The sculptures and the performance context give us strong evidence that music, dance and performance were important here."

"And what about the musical-pillar claim?"

"The form of the columns, the way vibration and resonance work, the mineral composition and crystal structure of the stone, the reported sounds, historical technical knowledge and modern research all give us evidence to investigate whether the acoustic design was deliberate."

"We should not claim more than the evidence allows. But we should not dismiss a claim just because it sounds surprising."

"That is scientific archaeology: ask a question, collect different kinds of evidence, test the idea, and make a conclusion based on the evidence."

"At the Stone Chariot, you will use the same way of thinking yourselves."

Section 5. Stone Chariot

Section Details

Duration: 10 mins

Led by: Lead Facilitator / Guide

Academic Focus: Claim, observation, interpretation, conclusion, independent evidence-based reasoning and comparison of evidence

Central Question: Can we test a claim using what we can actually observe?

Materials: A5 Stone Chariot Investigation Cards - 3 claim types, with enough copies for one card per small team.

Location

Stone Chariot, Vijaya Vittala Temple complex

Intent of the Section

To move students from guided inquiry to controlled independent investigation. The Guide or Lead Facilitator first models Claim > Observation > Interpretation > Conclusion using the monolith claim. Students then work in small teams to investigate a different claim themselves using visible archaeological evidence.

Instructions for Facilitator / Guide

  1. This section is delivered separately within each guide group of approximately 20 students. It may be led by the assigned Guide or by the Lead Facilitator taking guide groups in staggered slots.
  2. After Investigation 1, divide the guide group into teams of approximately five students. Give each team one A5 Investigation Card. There are three claim types; where four teams are formed, duplicate one of the cards.
  3. Students do not need to write. Each team investigates its claim orally using the prompts on the card: Observe, Interpret, Conclude and Defend.
  4. Allow 2-3 minutes for the team investigation. The Guide or Lead Facilitator may help students position themselves so they can see the relevant part of the monument and may use simple evidence prompts, but should not reveal the answer.
  5. After the investigation, bring the guide group back together and process the claims as a group.

Facilitator / Guide Script

Introduction to the Stone Chariot

“We have now reached the Stone Chariot.”

“In Vijayanagara, the chariot was an important part of religious and public life. During festivals, the idol could be taken out of the temple in a large wooden chariot and pulled through the streets.”

“This particular structure is a shrine dedicated to Garuda, the vahana of Vittala. Its architecture takes the form of a chariot.”

Introducing the Method

“You've just worked through three very different parts of Vittala.”

“Let's see if you noticed that you were actually doing different kinds of archaeological thinking.”

“At the gopura, what was the main problem you were trying to think about?”

Take 1 response.

“Yes, conservation. What is original, what has been repaired, and what happens when we replace old material.”

“At the Kalyana Mantapa, what were you looking at closely?”

Take 1–2 responses.

“Right. You observed sculptures and details, and then you tried to interpret what they might tell us.”

“At the Ranga Mantapa, you started with a claim. What did you need in order to investigate it?”

Take 1–2 responses.

“Evidence. And not just one kind of evidence.”

“Now I want to give you a simple sequence that brings all of this together.”

“Claim > Observation > Interpretation > Conclusion.”

“You start with a claim. You look for what you can actually observe. You interpret what those observations might mean. Then you decide what conclusion the evidence allows you to make.”

“I will show you the process once. Then you will use it yourselves in small teams.”

Investigation 1 - Is the Stone Chariot a Monolith?

Claim

“Here is our first claim: The Stone Chariot is carved from one enormous piece of stone.”

“Looking at it, does that seem possible?”

Take a few responses, but do not confirm the answer.

Observation

Ask students to inspect the structure carefully from bottom to top and look for places where one piece of stone meets another.

Guide students towards noticing separate blocks, joints, wheels, platform and upper shrine structure. Once enough evidence has been identified, move on.

Interpretation

“If we can identify separate stone blocks and joints, what does that tell us about the way this structure was made?”

Take 1–2 responses.

“It tells us that the Stone Chariot was not carved from one enormous block. It was constructed from multiple stone components fitted together to create one structure.”

Conclusion

“So, does the evidence support our original claim?”

Take 1 response.

“No. The visible joints and separate stone components show us that the Stone Chariot is not a monolith.”

“That is the process we just used: we started with a claim, made observations, interpreted the evidence, and reached a conclusion.”

Investigation 2 - Team Investigations

Set Up

“Now you are going to use the same method yourselves.”

“I am going to divide you into small teams. Each team will receive an Investigation Card with one claim.”

“Your job is not to guess the answer. Use the monument as your evidence.”

“Follow the sequence on the card: Observe. Interpret. Conclude. Defend.”

“At the end, be ready to tell us your claim, your conclusion, and your strongest piece of evidence.”

Divide the guide group into teams of approximately five and hand one A5 Investigation Card to each team.

Claim Cards

Card 1: “The wheels of the Stone Chariot could turn, but the Stone Chariot itself remained stationary.”

Card 2: “The Stone Chariot is drawn by a pair of elephants.”

Card 3: “The Stone Chariot was once painted in bright colours.”

Where there are four teams, duplicate one of the three cards so that every team has a claim to investigate.

Independent Investigation

Give teams 2–3 minutes to examine the monument and discuss their claim. Students should remain within the permitted viewing areas and must not touch the monument.

The Guide or Lead Facilitator circulates between teams without revealing the answer. Use only simple prompts when needed:

“What can you actually see?”

“What is your evidence?”

“Is that an observation or an assumption?”

“Could there be another explanation?”

Group Synthesis

Bring the teams back together within the guide group.

“Each team, tell us three things: your claim, your conclusion, and your strongest piece of evidence.”

Hear from every team. Process one claim at a time. Where two teams investigated the same claim, compare the evidence they noticed and whether they reached the same conclusion.

Land the Method

“Archaeology does not mean guessing. It means being clear about what you observed, what you interpreted, and how strongly the evidence supports your conclusion.”

“Sometimes the evidence is strong enough for a confident conclusion. Sometimes the most accurate answer is that we need more evidence.”

“Claim. Observation. Interpretation. Conclusion. You have now used the process yourselves.”

Section 6. Purandara Dasa Mantapa

Section Details

Duration: 30 mins

Led by: Facilitators

Academic Focus: None

Location

Purandara Dasa Mantapa

Intent of the Section

To provide students with a short recreational break after the academic component of the Vittala visit, while maintaining safe and organised group movement.

Facilitator Script

“We have finished our Vittala session. You now have about 30 minutes here to relax and take a break.”

“Stay within the area designated by your facilitators, and please don’t move towards the water or away from the group.”

“You can sit, have some water and take a short break. We’ll let you know when it is time to regroup.”

During the Break

Facilitators should:

  • Keep the group within the designated safe area.
  • Monitor students continuously.
  • Ensure students do not approach unsafe areas or the water’s edge.
  • Allow students to rest and have water.
  • Keep track of the time.
  • Give students a clear warning before the break ends.

Regrouping

A few minutes before departure:

“Alright everyone, let’s start getting back together. Please finish up, collect your belongings and come back to the group.”

Once everyone has regrouped:

  • Conduct a headcount.
  • Check that students have their journals, pens, water bottles and personal belongings.
  • Confirm that the group is ready for departure.
  • Coordinate the next movement with the guide/transport team as required.

Section 7. Return Journey — Purandara Dasa Mantapa to Bus

Section Details

Duration: 30 mins

Led by: Facilitators

Academic Focus: None

Location

Purandara Dasa Mantapa → buggy boarding area → Vittala parking → bus

Intent of the Section

To safely and efficiently move the group from the final recreational stop back to the bus while maintaining group integrity and completing the required headcounts and belongings checks.

Facilitator Script

“Alright everyone, our break is over. Please collect all your belongings and get ready to move.

Stay together and follow the facilitators. We are going back to the buggy area.”

Before beginning the walk:

  • Ensure all students have collected their belongings.
  • Conduct a headcount.
  • Confirm that the group is ready to move.
  • Facilitators position themselves at the front, middle and rear of the group as appropriate.

Moving Towards the Buggy Line

“Please stay in one line and keep moving with the group. Don’t stop along the way or move ahead of the facilitators.”

Facilitators should ensure that the group moves without disrupting the walking traffic.

Buggy Boarding

At the buggy line:

  • Maintain the student line.
  • Coordinate boarding across the multiple buggies.
  • Ensure approximately 10 students per buggy.
  • Ensure one adult is present in each buggy.
  • The lead facilitator monitors the line and coordinates the movement of students into the buggies.
  • Students should remain seated and with their designated adult throughout the transfer.

At Vittala Parking

Once the buggies reach the parking area:

“Stay together as you get down. We are going directly to the bus.”

Facilitators should:

  • Ensure students get down safely.
  • Reform the group immediately.
  • Move students directly towards the bus.
  • Prevent students from dispersing around the parking area.

Boarding the Bus

Before boarding:

“Before you get onto the bus, quickly check that you have everything with you—your journal, pen, water bottle and personal belongings.”

Once students are seated:

  • Conduct the final headcount.
  • Ensure all students are seated appropriately.
  • Confirm that the group is complete before the bus departs.
