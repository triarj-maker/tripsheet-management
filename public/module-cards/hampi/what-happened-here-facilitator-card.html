What Happened Here? - Facilitator Card

Module Snapshot

Duration: 15–20 minutes
Location: Indoor activity space
Team Size: 5–8 students
Grade Range: Grades 7-12
Module Type: Inquiry Activity
Program Use: Can sit within Introduction & Context Setting, Archaeology 101, or be used independently.

Materials Checklist:

  • Round 1 Evidence Sheet
  • Round 2 Stratigraphy Reveal
  • Plain A4 sheets
  • Pens / pencils
  • Board or paper for debrief drawing

Purpose

To demonstrate that different narratives can emerge from the same evidence .

Students investigate the remains of a fictional medieval building in groups and construct a narrative from archaeological evidence.

Different people can interpret the same evidence differently. Interpretations must still be supported by evidence, and new evidence can change what we think happened.

The activity opens the possibility of discussing how evidence can be interpreted to build narratives.

Must-Land Ideas

  • The same evidence can produce different interpretations.
  • Interpretations need to be supported by evidence.
  • Archaeological context changes what evidence can tell us.
  • New evidence can change a narrative.
  • Sometimes the available evidence simply does not allow us to know.

Activity Flow

1. Introduce the Mystery | 1 min

Give each team the Round 1 Evidence Sheet.

SCRIPT:

“Archaeologists have uncovered the remains of a building in a medieval town. Your team must investigate the evidence and reconstruct what you think happened here.”

Ask them to consider:

  • What kind of place was this?
  • Who might have lived or worked here?
  • What happened to the building?

Tell each team to agree on one narrative.

Note: It is important for facilitators to understand that there is no single correct answer for this activity. The evidence is ambiguous on purpose so that several interpretations can emerge. So do not force your interpretation on the students, let them interpret it anyway they want. That is the whole point of this activity.

2. Investigate & Build a Narrative | 4–5 min

Teams study the evidence and discuss their explanation.

They may use a plain A4 sheet to record:

  • their narrative
  • the strongest evidence supporting it

Facilitator Does:

Walk around the room, look at how each group is interpreting the evidence. Make a mental note of groups that you’d like to share later.

Facilitator Prompts

Use only neutral prompts:

  • “What do you notice?”
  • “What makes you think that?”
  • “What evidence supports your explanation?”
  • “What does this tell you about the people who used this place?”

Do not suggest possible explanations.

If students make claims about what happened to the occupants, ask:

“What evidence tells us that?”

It is perfectly acceptable for the answer to be:

“We don't know.”

3. Compare Narratives | 2–3 min

Ask 2 or 3 teams with different explanations to share briefly in less than 30 secs.

Then reveal:

“Every team received exactly the same evidence.”

Ask:

“So why did we arrive at different stories?”

Allow a few responses. Do not explain fully yet.

4. Reveal New Evidence | 2 min

Give teams the Round 2 Stratigraphy Reveal.

Ask:

“Where were the arrowheads actually found?”

“Were they in the same layer as the final fire?”

Students should recognise that the arrowheads belong to an earlier occupation layer.

Ask:

“What does that change?”

Do not say that the attack explanation is “wrong”. The new evidence only means the arrowheads cannot be used as direct evidence for an attack during the final fire.

5. Rebuild the Narrative | 1–2 min

Ask:

“Does your explanation still hold, or does it need to change?”

Teams can keep, modify or replace their narrative.

Debrief | 4–5 min

SCRIPT:

“What we just saw is something really important. Every team had the same evidence, but you did not all arrive at the same explanation.

And this happens all the time when we study the past.

We cannot go back and watch exactly what happened hundreds or thousands of years ago. What we have are pieces of evidence that survived.”

Draw several dots on a board or sheet.

“Imagine each dot is one piece of evidence. We try to connect those dots and build a picture of what might have happened.

But we don't always have all the dots.”

Add another dot.

“Sometimes new evidence appears. Or we discover that one of the dots we were using actually belongs somewhere else.

That is what happened with the arrowheads. Once we discovered that they came from an older layer, the meaning of that evidence changed.”

Then land:

“History is our reconstruction and interpretation of the past, based on the evidence that survives.”

Continue:

“But interpretation does not mean we can make up any story we want. We still have to ask:

What evidence supports this?

Where did that evidence come from?

What else could it mean?

How certain can we be?

And sometimes the most accurate answer is simply: we don't know yet.”

“These are questions we are going to keep coming back to as we investigate Hampi.”

Facilitator Watch-Outs

  • Do not lead students toward a particular narrative.
  • Do not describe this as a puzzle with one correct answer.
  • Keep Round 2 completely hidden until teams have committed to Round 1.
  • Do not imply that the arrowheads prove an attack.
  • Do not reveal what caused the fire. The evidence does not establish this.
  • Do not conclude what happened to the occupants.
  • Different interpretations do not mean every explanation is equally valid. Ask for evidence.
  • The goal is reasoning and revision, not finding the answer.

Success Check

The activity has landed if students understand that:

  • the same evidence can lead to different interpretations
  • they need evidence to support a narrative
  • context can change the meaning of an artefact
  • new evidence may require them to revise their thinking
  • some questions about the past remain uncertain
